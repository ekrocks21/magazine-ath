We did not watch this on a livestream. We have a livestream. We built it, it points at this exact water, and for four days in July we ignored it, because the only place to be that week was out on the bay with the engine running.

What follows is what those four days were actually like from a small boat on the Jersey side — the waiting, the weather, the moment a two-hundred-and-fifty-foot barque comes past close enough that you stop filming and just look at it.

## The scale

The scale is worth establishing, because it is hard to overstate and easy to nod past.

Sail4th 250 was the official maritime celebration of the Semiquincentennial. Forty-eight tall ships from twenty foreign nations. Roughly fifty gray-hull naval vessels in the Hudson for the Seventh International Naval Review, one of the rarest ceremonies the Navy performs. More than a hundred aircraft overhead, led by the Blue Angels. Count every flag in the harbor that week — ships, vessels, aircraft, crews — and you are past forty nations. Organizers called it the largest peacetime maritime gathering in American history and nobody has seriously argued otherwise.

The tradition runs back to Operation Sail in 1964, from an idea President Kennedy endorsed in 1961: that gathering the world's sailing ships in peace could do something diplomacy could not. Then 1976 for the Bicentennial. 1986 for the Statue of Liberty. 1992, 2000, 2012. Every time this country hits a number that matters, the ships come.

And they come through here. That is not local pride, it is geography. New York Harbor has one deepwater approach and it runs past Sandy Hook, which means the fleet had to cross the mouth of our bay to get anywhere. Governor Sherrill called this stretch the gateway to America. She was describing our commute.

We watched the bay stay empty for weeks. Then it stopped being empty.

## Day one

The first day you do not do anything clever. You just go look.

We ran out from Highlands in the morning and the masts came up over the horizon before the hulls did, which is a thing you have read about and then see happen and understand differently. They were anchored off Sandy Hook, spread across the Lower Bay, waiting for their turn at the harbor. From a distance a fleet at anchor looks like a small city that has been assembled overnight out of rope.

We spent that day going ship to ship, slow, no plan beyond proximity. Argentina's *Libertad*. Spain's *Juan Sebastián de Elcano*. Italy's *Amerigo Vespucci*, which is the most beautiful working ship in the world and does not particularly care whether you agree. The USCGC *Eagle*.

Two things surprised us.

The first is how quiet it is. A tall ship at anchor makes almost no sound. You cut your engine forty yards off and what you get is water, rigging, and occasionally a voice in a language you do not speak carrying farther across flat water than it has any right to.

The second is the crews. They wave. All of them, constantly, from every ship, at every small boat that comes near — young sailors hanging off the rail, taking pictures of us taking pictures of them. We had braced for spectacle and got something closer to a neighborhood.

That evening the sky went bad. A severe line came through Monmouth County with seventy-mile-an-hour gusts and penny hail, put something like a hundred and twenty-one thousand people in the dark, and dropped trees across the North Jersey Coast Line at Red Bank. We were off the water by then, watching it come across the bay from the hill.

Forty-eight ships rode it out at anchor. In the morning they were all still there.

## Day two

The second day is the one people ask about.

Captain Ryan wake surfed in front of the *Eagle*.

Written flat like that it sounds like a stunt, and honestly, at the time, it partly was. But the reason it is the center of this story and the center of our film is that of every hull in that fleet, the *Eagle* is the one carrying the strangest history, and we did not fully know it when we were out there grinning at her.

She was built in Hamburg in 1936 as the *Horst Wessel*, a sail training ship for Nazi Germany, launched under the flag of a regime that named her after one of its martyrs. She survived the war. The United States took her in 1946 as war reparation — the ship was found half-sunk in the Weser — sailed her across the Atlantic, and put her to work training the United States Coast Guard. The swastika that had ridden her bow was cut off and replaced with a hand-carved teak shield.

Ninety years on, she is America's Tall Ship. Every Coast Guard officer of the modern era has learned the sea on a hull built for the Third Reich. There is no cleaner object lesson anywhere on the water: the thing itself did not change, the hands on it did.

We were in her wake with a speaker playing Springsteen. There is a version of this article that treats that as irreverent. We would argue the opposite. A ship built for one of the worst governments in human history now spends its summers being surfed behind by a guy from Monmouth County while her crew waves from the rail. That is the whole American argument in one frame, and it was accidental, and we have it on camera.

The silhouettes that afternoon were the best light of the week — sails stacked into the sun, ships going black against it, the water coming up gold. We stopped talking for a while. It is in the footage. You can hear the engine and nothing else.

## Day three

Day three was the parade of sail, sixteen miles of it, from near Sandy Hook up to the George Washington Bridge.

We ran with it as long as we were allowed.

This is the day the event stopped being ours and became everyone's. The water filled. Thousands of boats, flags on every one of them, a spontaneous civilian flotilla forming up alongside the official column. People who had never met each other running parallel courses at the same speed for hours. Somebody's kid on a bowrider waving at a Polish barque.

We anchored at the southwest corner of the Verrazzano and watched it come through underneath. The naval vessels. The tall ships in column. The Blue Angels overhead with a hundred aircraft behind them. Standing on a small boat in the shadow of that bridge while the world's navies pass beneath it is not a thing this magazine can describe adequately and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Eventually the Coast Guard turned us back, which was correct, and which we mention because it is true and because we would rather be accurate than sound like we had more access than we did.

We came home down the bay in the evening with the light behind us. Every boat we passed was heading the same way. Nobody was in a hurry.

## Day four

On the last day we went back up the harbor on the *Nicole Evelyn*, a hundred and fifty-seven feet, owned by a Highlands family, and we saw the fleet one more time from a deck considerably better appointed than our own.

It was a strange, good, valedictory day. The ships were tied up along the piers by then and you could see them the way you see a thing that is about to leave. We made the loop. Statue, harbor, the whole postcard, except the postcard had forty-eight tall ships in it.

Then we came home, and the last day turned out not to be about the harbor at all.

The sailors of the *Amerigo Vespucci* came to Highlands.

They came up into a town of about five thousand people that mostly makes its living from the water and always has, and the town turned out for them. Italian sailors in dress whites on Bay Avenue. People pulling them into restaurants. Somebody's grandmother speaking Italian for the first time in years to a nineteen-year-old from Livorno who was a very long way from home.

That was the end of it, and it happened three blocks from our house.

## The front door

Here is what we keep coming back to, months later.

Everyone watched this event from Manhattan. That was the broadcast, that was the photograph, that is what it will look like in the archive. And it is a real vantage — nobody is arguing the Battery is a bad place to stand.

<aside class="article-pullquote">
  <blockquote>The harbor has a front door, and the front door is this bay. The world came in through our threshold, four days running, and the people who live here got to be first.</blockquote>
</aside>

Every one of those forty-eight ships crossed the water off Miller Beach before anyone in New York saw a single mast.

We have said all year that the season arrives here before it arrives anywhere else, and that the only real job is to be watching when it does. In July we got the biggest possible version of that. Forty-eight ships, twenty nations, and a bay we look at every morning suddenly carrying the whole thing.

They will not be back in our lifetime. The next one of these is somebody else's story.

We were on the water for all four days of ours.
