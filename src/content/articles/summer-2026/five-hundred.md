On the last Sunday in July, a humpback lunged through a school of bunker off Sandy Hook with its mouth open and forty feet of the Atlantic came up with it. The boat that saw it was carrying paying passengers out of Belmar. The whale went down, came up again, and did it twice more. It was a Sunday afternoon on one of the busiest stretches of coastline in the country, and there was a whale feeding in it.

That is not a rare sentence anymore. It used to be.

## Five hundred, one tail at a time

In late July, Gotham Whale — a Staten Island nonprofit that has been keeping the New York City Humpback Whale Catalog since 2012 — logged the five hundredth individual humpback documented in the waters around the five boroughs and down our coast.

Five hundred distinct animals, each one identified the way the researchers have always done it: by the tail.

A humpback's fluke is a fingerprint. The trailing edge nicks and notches differently on every animal, and the white pigment on the underside is a pattern no other whale carries. Photograph the fluke as the animal dives, match it against the catalog, and you know not just that a whale was here, but *which* whale was here — and whether it was here last year, and the year before that. Some of the animals in the catalog have been followed from juveniles into adulthood. They are not passing through. They are coming back.

The scale of the change is easier to see in the annual counts than in the milestone. Before 2018, the network logged something like ten to thirty whales in a season. In recent years it has run as high as a hundred.

## Why they came back

Why did they come back? The short answer is bunker.

Atlantic menhaden — bunker, mossbunker, pogy, depending on which dock you learned the word on — is a small oily filter feeder that swims in schools thick enough to darken the water. It is the fish nobody eats and everybody depends on. Bluefish eat it. Stripers eat it. Ospreys eat it. Bluefin tuna eat it. And humpback whales, which need to move an enormous amount of calories through a short feeding season, will follow a bunker school straight into the mouth of a harbor.

For most of the twentieth century, the bunker were not here in those numbers, and neither was anything that eats them. Coastwide catch limits changed that. So did four decades of slow, unglamorous water quality work — wastewater treatment plants, combined sewer overflow projects, the long tail of the Clean Water Act — running quietly in the background of every harbor town on this bay. Cleaner water coming down the Hudson and out of the tributaries. More oxygen. More forage. Nobody held a ribbon cutting for it. It took a generation.

The whales themselves are here for a second reason, and it is less cheerful. The traditional northern feeding grounds — the Gulf of Maine, the water off Cape Cod — are warming faster than most of the ocean, and the prey species are redistributing. As Gotham Whale's Chris St. Lawrence put it, these whales are "looking for new food sources." What they found was us. Or rather, what they found was a stretch of coast that has spent forty years becoming worth eating in again.

Both things are true at once. The harbor got cleaner, and the north got warmer, and the whales split the difference and came to the Bight.

## The longer list

The whales get the headlines because they are enormous and they breach in front of the skyline. But they are the top of a list that has been quietly getting longer on this bay for years, and if you live here you have watched it happen without necessarily filing it as news.

Bottlenose dolphins now push into Sandy Hook Bay in pods that stop conversation on the ferry dock. Ospreys nest on channel markers we grew up seeing empty. Bald eagles have come back to the Meadowlands, of all places, on the strength of clean water and forage fish. Seals haul out on the Hook in winter. Cownose rays, bluefin, sharks close enough in to make the local news each August.

None of that is an accident, and none of it is separate. It is one system, and the fish at the bottom of it is the one nobody has ever ordered in a restaurant.

## The warning attached

The milestone came with a warning attached, and it belongs in this piece as much as the number does. Danielle Brown, Gotham Whale's director of research, noted that "with more whales comes more overlap with human activities."

That overlap is not theoretical here. This is the Port of New York and New Jersey, the busiest container port on the East Coast, laid over the same water as a summer's worth of small boats, ferries, charters, and personal watercraft. A meaningful share of the animals in the catalog carry propeller scars or the evidence of ship strikes. The whale that lunge-feeds in front of a phone camera at four in the afternoon is doing it inside a shipping lane.

We say this as people who are on that water constantly and with engines running. Slow down when the birds pile up. A working flock of gulls and gannets over a boiling patch of bunker is not a photo opportunity, it is a dinner table, and something large may be coming up through the middle of it. Give the animal room. Report what you see — Gotham Whale takes sightings from anyone in the Bight, from Montauk to Cape May, and a dated photograph of a fluke from a guy on a center console is a real data point in a real catalog.

## The longest timeline

We have said all season that this coast is the first door — that the ships came through it in July, that the season arrives here before it arrives anywhere else, that the only real job is to be watching when it does.

The whales are the version of that story with the longest timeline. They did not come back because of anything that happened this summer. They came back because of decisions made in the 1970s by people who never saw the result, and because of catch limits argued over in fishery meetings nobody covered, and because a hundred small unphotogenic pieces of infrastructure did their work in the dark for forty years.

Five hundred animals, one fluke at a time, cataloged by volunteers on charter boats with cameras.

It is alive, and it has been paying attention to what we do.
