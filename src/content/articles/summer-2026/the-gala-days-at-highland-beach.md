There is a house at the foot of the Highlands Bridge that most people barely notice. They drive over the bridge, off the span, down the ramp toward Sandy Hook, the ocean opening up on the left, and they pass within a hundred feet of it without knowing it is there. It stands in the shadow of the ramp, white cedar shake gone gray, windows boarded, a two-story square frame house with a hip roof and a bay front, facing a road that brings you in and out of the park.

It is the last building of a resort that once drew tens of thousands of people to this exact stretch of sand. It is the only thing the state did not take down.

Everything else at Highland Beach — including the dance pavilion, the bathing houses, the roller coaster, the bamboo theater a man built from reeds he carried back from Cuba, the hundred cabanas, the confectionary, the pier where the steamboats from Manhattan came in — all of it is gone, torn down or hauled off or buried under the dune grass that grew back over the footprints. What remains is the house and a cracked concrete path, and almost no one under sixty who remembers why either one is there.

This is the story of Highland Beach. For seventy-four years it was the first place on the Jersey Shore — not the biggest, not the most famous, but among the first, the nearest ocean beach to New York that a working family could reach in an afternoon and be home by dark. It is the story of the Sandlass family, who ran it across three generations. And it is the story of how it ended, which is a quieter and more instructive story than how it began.

Almost no one remembers it. That is not an accident. Erasure is a thing that happens to places, and it usually happens on purpose.

## The geography is the whole reason

Let's start with the geography, because the geography is the whole reason.

The southern end of the Sandy Hook peninsula is a barrier beach barely wider than a road. At Highland Beach the Atlantic Ocean and the Shrewsbury River sit fifty yards apart — surf on one side, calm river on the other, close enough that a family could bathe in the waves in the morning and the still water in the afternoon without moving the picnic. For a New Yorker in the 1880s, this was the closest thing the coast offered to a guaranteed good day.

The timing was the thing. This was the dawn of leisure time in America — the first moment when ordinary people had both a day off and a way to travel on it. A December 1887 notice in the Red Bank Register laid out what was coming: a new depot, and bathing, music, and dancing pavilions, stores, pagodas in unusual styles, hotels. By 1888 the Great Switchback Railroad — an early roller coaster, the first ride on the property — was already carrying people down its wooden hills. Cottages were going up on the northern lots by 1889. The place, in the language of the day, was booming.

<aside class="article-pullquote">
  <blockquote>Highland Beach is an excursion resort, especially designed for family parties. The bathing is especially fine and varied, the ocean and the river being but fifty yards apart.</blockquote>
  <cite>— Promotional description, c. 1888</cite>
</aside>

That phrase — *excursion resort* — is the key to everything Highland Beach was and everything it stopped being. It was not built for the wealthy who summered for a season. It was built for the day-tripper: the family that came for one day, spent what it had, and went home. Its entire existence depended on being easy to reach, and that dependence is what eventually killed it.

You came by water, or you came by rail, and for a while you could come by both.

Steamboat travel to the Highlands went back to 1832 and the *Saratoga*. By the resort's peak, large steamers ran down from Manhattan, tied up at a pier the proprietor kept lengthening as the river's current shifted the channel, and let out crowds onto the beach. The boat ride itself was part of the day — down the harbor, past the Hook, the whole coast opening up ahead.

Then came the railroad, and the railroad changed the scale of the thing. In 1892 the Navesink Railroad Company built a new rail and pedestrian bridge across the Shrewsbury and ran a line out to a pier at Atlantic Highlands, within three miles of the resort. Steamboats from the city could now meet the rail extension, or smaller steam launches could ferry passengers the last stretch. A criss-cross bridge carried trains, wagons, and walkers across the river and deposited them in the middle of the resort. Anticipating the crowds this would bring, Highland Beach added a hundred bathhouses. Then, a few years later, two hundred more.

For a New York family, the trip was now genuinely simple: a boat down the harbor, a short train across the river, and you stepped off into music and salt air. That accessibility was the resort's entire proposition. Remember it. When it went away, so did everything else.

## The name on the place was Sandlass

The name on the place, for most of its life, was Sandlass.

William T. Sandlass, Jr. — "Will" to his family — first got involved in November 1887, leasing acreage from the developer Ferdinand Fish. On January 5, 1893, the Highland Beach Improvement Company leased him Lots 23 through 26 for five years at five hundred dollars a year. He did not own the ground under his own resort until March 18, 1920, when he finally bought the lots outright. But from that first lease forward, the place was his in every way that mattered.

He was, by every account, a tinkerer — a man who could not leave the place alone, who rebuilt or added or improved something nearly every season. In 1897 the Monmouth Press reported that he had extended his steamboat pier seventy-five feet into the river because the current had moved the channel and stranded last year's dock. That is the whole man in one sentence: the water changed, so he changed the pier.

And there is one improvement that this magazine cannot pass by. Sometime in the early twentieth century, Will Sandlass traveled to Cuba and came home with a considerable quantity of bamboo. He used it to build a set of structures unlike anything else on the Jersey coast — the Bamboo Garden, the Bamboo Bar, and, at the center of it, an open-air theater of a kind that was popular around the First World War.

They called it the Airdrome.

An open-air theater, built of Cuban bamboo, on the southern tip of Sandy Hook, sometime around 1915. They called it the Airdrome. The name has been waiting on this coast for a hundred years.

The Sandlass family lived above the Bamboo Bar, in the frame house that sat between the railroad track and the roadway. That house — built in 1893, its internal framing timbers salvaged from the dismantled 1889 gravity roller coaster — is the one still standing at the foot of the bridge today. The National Park Service calls it Building 600.

By around 1910, the resort had become a full landscape of pleasure. Photographs and postcards from the years on either side of the First World War show a Merry-Go-Round, the Basket Pavilion — a dance hall — the Bamboo Garden with its theater, a shooting gallery, a bar, storefronts, and the confectionary. Down the river side ran the cabanas; along the northern lots stood the private summer cottages that families leased from Sandlass and returned to year after year, forming friendships and traditions that outlasted the buildings.

Picture the day from the water, the way most visitors first saw it — the approach a steamer made coming down from New York. The Twin Lights on the hill above Highlands. The long pier reaching out into the Shrewsbury. Then the beach itself resolving into detail: the sweep of bathhouses, the pavilions, the sound of the band carrying out over the water before the boat had even tied up. This was the shore before the shore had competitors. For a generation of New Yorkers, going to the beach meant going here.

The bathing was the main event — the ocean on one side, the calmer river on the other, and the crowd moving between them all day. But the resort sold a full day's worth of everything: the coaster, the carousel, dancing in the pavilion, a film in the bamboo theater as the light went down, food, drink, and the long ride home in the dark with sand still on your feet.

And it was not only families. The place drew, in its glory years, a glitter of visitors from Broadway and the early silent-film world — a fashionable, slightly theatrical crowd that gave the gala days their particular shine. Highland Beach was never grand in the Newport sense. It was something better suited to this coast: democratic, a little rowdy, open to anyone who could afford the boat.

For a generation, the Jersey Shore was not a hundred towns strung down a hundred miles of coast. It was one beach, at the end of one boat ride, and this was it.

## The storms

No account of this place is honest without the storms.

A barrier beach fifty yards wide is a fragile address. Every year the ocean came for it, and some years the ocean nearly won. An October 1896 storm cut a broad inlet five or six feet deep clean through the beach from the surf to the river, the ocean poured through the gap all night, carried off the north end of the bathhouse row, and, in one witness's account, tossed the great protective rocks along the oceanfront about like playthings.

The Sandlass family lived with this as a permanent condition — building, rebuilding, moving what the sea threatened. The Great Hurricane of September 21, 1938 did enough damage that they moved the family house the following year, south and closer to the river, to safer ground. The house at the foot of the bridge has already survived more than most buildings on this coast will ever face. It is worth remembering that when you look at it standing there in the dark under the ramp.

## What actually killed it

Here is the part worth paying attention to, because it is the part that repeats.

Highland Beach was not killed by a storm. It was not killed by fire or by a bad season or by the family losing interest. It was killed by the thing that built it — access — running in reverse.

The whole resort had been engineered around the boat and the train. Then, slowly, the country stopped traveling that way. Steamboat service faded; the *Little Silver* made her last run to the scrapyard in 1932. Rail service to the shore was discontinued in the late 1940s. And in the 1950s the New Jersey Turnpike and the Garden State Parkway opened, and the automobile became the way everyone reached the coast.

The car changed the geometry of the entire shore. When you came by boat or train, the nearest beach won, and the nearest beach was Highland Beach. When you came by car, distance stopped mattering the way it had — a family could keep driving, past the Hook, down through the towns strung along the Parkway, and pick from a hundred beaches that had not existed as options before. The advantage that had built Highland Beach — closest to the city — simply evaporated. You cannot be the nearest beach when nearness no longer decides anything.

<aside class="article-pullquote">
  <blockquote>The resort was built on being easy to reach. The moment everything became easy to reach, being easy to reach was worth nothing. That is not a shore story. That is every story.</blockquote>
</aside>

The Sandlass family adapted, the way Will always had. The day-trip resort became the Sandlass Beach Club — cabanas, a members' crowd drawn mostly from northern Monmouth County families rather than the Manhattan steamer. The tropical Bamboo Room ran as a nightclub from 1941 to 1961; teenagers still danced there on summer nights near the very end. The Victorian buildings came down one by one or were cut down into smaller, plainer, more useful shapes. The gala days were over, but the place held on.

## The state

What finished it was not the market. It was the state.

By 1960 the government wanted the southern end of Sandy Hook for a state park, and wanted the Sandlass land for access to it. On January 1, 1962, the Army and the State of New Jersey signed a lease turning the southern end of the Fort Hancock reservation into parkland. Then the state invoked eminent domain and took the family's property — Lots 23 through 33 — for the new park's approach.

The Sandlass family did not agree the state's offer was fair. They filed suit. On December 28, 1962, Henry J. and Helen J. Sandlass were awarded $350,000 in condemnation proceedings by the New Jersey Superior Court for the lots the state had taken.

Three hundred fifty thousand dollars for seventy-five summers. The buildings came down. In December 1974 the Army closed Fort Hancock and turned the whole peninsula, the old resort ground included, over to the Department of the Interior as part of the Gateway National Recreation Area. A new highway ramp went in off the bridge, and the front lawn of the Sandlass house — the lawn three generations had crossed — disappeared under it.

Drive onto Sandy Hook today and you pass through it without a marker to tell you. Sand dunes and dune grass have taken back nearly all of it. An obscure concrete path, once crowded, runs off toward nothing. And the house stands under the ramp — the 1893 frame house with the roller-coaster bones inside its walls, Building 600, the single surviving structure of the first resort on the Jersey Shore.

## What survives

It is not in good shape. The paint is long gone. But it is recognizably the same house that appears in the century-old photographs, and there are people working to keep it that way. Susan Sandlass Gardiner — a member of the family — co-founded the Jersey Coast Heritage Museum in 2016 to hold the memory of Highland Beach and wrote the definitive account of it, *Sandy Hook's Lost Highland Beach Resort*. The Twin Lights Museum keeps a room of the resort's artifacts. The Park Service holds the deeds and the photographs. The record survives, in other words, even where the buildings do not.

That is the archaeology of the gala days. Not nostalgia — nostalgia would ask you to miss it. This asks you only to know it was here: that the shore you drive to had a first door, and that the door was at the end of a boat ride, at the foot of a bamboo theater, on fifty yards of sand between the ocean and the river, and that a family named Sandlass held it open for seventy-five summers.

The house is still there. Now you will see it when you pass.

Look for it on your way to the beach.
