There is a specific double-take that happened up and down this coast in May, and if you were here you had it.

You come over the rise, or around the point, or out past the Hook, and the water is turquoise. Not the ordinary green the Atlantic goes when it warms. Not the brown the NYC rivers hand us after a hard rain.

Turquoise — bright, milky, banded, the color of somewhere you have to fly to. And your first thought is not *what a beautiful day*. Your first thought is that something is wrong, because this water does not do that.

It did it for weeks. And nothing was wrong.

## What was actually in it

The color was phytoplankton — microscopic plant-like organisms drifting near the surface, in concentrations high enough to change what the ocean does with light.

NASA satellites picked up the first patches in early April, off New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland, and Virginia. By May 3 the MODIS instrument aboard the Aqua satellite captured the whole thing as a set of enormous swirling ribbons running along the Mid-Atlantic coast. The bloom held from Virginia north through the Jersey Shore. It was, in the plainest sense, visible from space.

Each individual organism is around fifty microns — smaller than a human hair, invisible to you standing in it up to your knees. There were simply enough of them to matter optically.

Here is the mechanism, which is more interesting than the color.

Phytoplankton contain chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs parts of the sunlight that hits it and reflects others — specifically the blue-green wavelengths. Water dense with phytoplankton therefore throws back a color the open Atlantic does not usually show. The turquoise was not the water. It was several billion organisms doing photosynthesis, all at once, in front of you.

## The ocean fertilized itself

Blooms need two things: light and nutrients. Spring supplies the light. The nutrients came up from below.

The engine is upwelling. When winds push surface water away from the coast, deeper water rises to replace it — colder, and loaded with the nutrients that accumulate down there. Sustained southwest winds along this coast do exactly that, and 2026 delivered them. Rutgers oceanographer Oscar Schofield, who has studied this water for decades, described it as a productive year with a lot of seasonal upwelling and phytoplankton blooms.

Which is a scientist's way of saying the ocean fertilized itself.

There is a detail here worth sitting with. NASA classifies our stretch of coast — the Mid-Atlantic Bight, running from Cape Hatteras up to Martha's Vineyard — as optically noisy. Some of the literature is less polite and calls it dirty. That is not an insult about pollution; it is a description of the water's visual complexity. Our rivers deliver constant plumes of suspended sediment and dissolved organic matter, which makes coastal color here genuinely difficult to read from orbit.

So the Atlantic off New Jersey is, by scientific classification, one of the murkiest stretches of coastal water on the eastern seaboard.

And this spring, it was the color of Bermuda.

## Color and clarity are two different things

Two different things got tangled together in the way people talked about this, and they are worth separating.

The color was the bloom — organisms in the water reflecting blue-green light back at you.

The clarity was mostly the wind. When west winds blow surface water offshore, they take a good deal of suspended material with it, and what is left is unusually legible. Lifeguards down the shore reported being able to watch fish swimming below the surface. That is a different phenomenon from the bloom, driven by the same winds. They arrived together this year, which is why the water looked less like the Atlantic than it has in living memory.

Phytoplankton is the bottom of everything. It is the base of the marine food web, the thing that converts sunlight into calories that the rest of the ocean can eventually eat. A bloom of this size is not decoration. It is a very large deposit into an account that the whole coast draws on.

Elsewhere in this issue we write about five hundred humpback whales now cataloged in the waters around New York, and about the bunker schools that brought them. This is the other end of that chain. The bunker filter-feed on the small stuff. The whales eat the bunker. The water you were photographing in May because it looked pretty was, functionally, the reason a humpback lunged through a bait ball off Sandy Hook in July.

## The honest read

A bloom is not automatically good news, and this magazine is not going to pretend otherwise just because it photographed well.

This one was benign. Scientists were consistent that it posed no meaningful hazard to swimmers, and that blooms of this kind are a normal spring feature of this coast, varying in timing and size from year to year. That is the honest read and we have no reason to soften it.

But productivity is not the same thing as health. Blooms fed by runoff and pollution rather than upwelling behave differently. And when a very large bloom dies off all at once, the decomposition can strip oxygen out of the water and kill things — the mechanism behind fish kills and dead zones elsewhere in this country. The same process that turns the water beautiful can, under different conditions, turn it lethal.

That is not a reason to have enjoyed it less. It is a reason to know the difference.

## After

By late summer the color had gone back to what it always is here, which is a workmanlike gray-green that no one flies anywhere to see.

The thing worth keeping is not the photograph. It is the reminder that this water — the murky, sediment-loaded, scientifically noisy Atlantic off one of the most developed coastlines on earth — is not a static backdrop. It is a system running at a scale we mostly cannot perceive, and every so often it does something so large that it becomes briefly, accidentally visible to us.

This is still New Jersey. That was the whole point.
