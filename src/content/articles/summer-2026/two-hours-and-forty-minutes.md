There is a street in this town that starts at the bay and does not stop. It comes up out of the water at Miller Beach, climbs the hill, crosses the highway, and keeps going under a different name. Our house is on the upper half of it. The lower half is where Trudy Ederle learned to swim.

We did not know that when we moved in.

She would start at Sandy Hook and swim to the Highlands bridge. Two hours and forty minutes, in the river she had learned in as a child, past the boathouses and the bathing pavilions and the steamboat piers, past whatever the tide was doing that day at the drawbridge. Then she would get out and do it again another day.

That is not a legend. That is training. And it happened here, in water you can see from Bay Avenue, a hundred years ago.

## The butcher's daughter

Gertrude Caroline Ederle was born in Manhattan in October 1905, the third of six children of German immigrants. Her father Henry ran a butcher shop on Amsterdam Avenue. The family lived around the business in the West Sixties, in a New York that was loud, crowded, and entirely indoors.

And every summer, they came here.

The Ederles kept a summer cottage in Highlands. That sentence gets repeated in every article ever written about her, usually in passing, on the way to the Channel. It deserves more than a clause. For a butcher's family from the West Side, Highlands was the whole year's reward — a bay, a river, a beach, and enough distance from Manhattan to feel like a different life while still being close enough to reach on a boat.

She learned to swim at the beach on Miller Street.

It is worth being specific about where that is, because it is not an abstraction and it is not gone. Miller Beach sits at the corner of Shrewsbury Avenue and Miller Street, at the bottom of the hill, where the street runs out into the water. Miller Street was one of the spines of this town from the beginning — the first public school in Highlands was built at Miller and Navesink, the first Methodist church stood on Miller near Shore Drive, the first Our Lady of Perpetual Help went up at Navesink and Miller, and when the town's only water came down by gravity from the springs in the hills, it came down to Miller Street first and out to the rest of town from there.

The street climbs from the beach and keeps climbing. In an older Highlands, the houses on the hillside all had long flights of steps running down to the business district below, which is how people got to the water: on foot, downhill, every day.

A few of those staircases are still here, in peanut stone, public and unmarked, if you know where to look. They are the last working pieces of the town she walked down through — not a monument, not a plaque, just the actual stairs, still doing the job they were cut for.

The story that has come down about the first swim is that her father took her out to the Patten Line pier, tied a clothesline around her waist, and lowered her into water over her head — which sounds alarming now and was, at the time, simply how it was done. Her sisters were already in. She was afraid, and then she was not, and then she was paddling.

She was around nine years old. Four years earlier, at five, she had come through a case of measles that left her with an ear infection and permanent hearing damage. It would get worse across her life. She swam anyway, in a river, with a rope around her middle, and eventually without it.

She joined the Women's Swimming Association after seeing a group of WSA girls give an exhibition near the family's cottage — which is to say that the single most consequential decision of her athletic life was made on a beach in this town, watching other women swim.

## The town she trained in

It is worth stopping the story here and looking around, because the Highlands she trained in was not the Highlands you are standing in.

The 1920s were the peak of the steamboat era on this water, and it is difficult to overstate the scale of it. Side-wheelers ran multiple round trips a day between New York and the river towns. In 1926 alone — the year of the Channel — two steamboat companies reported carrying more than a million passengers between New York and New Jersey. The Patten Line boats, the *Thomas Patten*, the *Mary Patten*, the *Little Silver*, the *Elberon*, were the commuter rail of the Shrewsbury, and Highlands was a stop on the line.

They came for what was here. Hotels, merry-go-rounds, picnic groves, and dancing pavilions lined the shores of the Shrewsbury at Highlands and Sandy Hook. There had been a floating theater on the river that put on Gilbert and Sullivan for audiences in the thousands. Twenty-six passenger trains a day ran through neighboring Atlantic Highlands. The bridge to Highland Beach had opened the resort to more than a hundred thousand visitors in a single season a generation earlier, and the crowds had not stopped.

Across the river sat Highland Beach itself — the Sandlass resort, which readers of this issue have already met in these pages. Bathhouses, a beach platform, a photographic gallery where you could pose for a souvenir. The Bamboo Garden where people danced at night to violin, flute, and piano under Japanese parasols. A walkway ran from the Bamboo Garden down to the old Patten Line pier near the government reservation.

That is the pier in the family story. That is where the clothesline went in the water.

The visitors were New Yorkers, mostly — day trippers and season renters off the boats, families escaping the heat, the same trade that had been coming since the post-Civil War boom. Highlands was a fishing and clamming town that put on a summer face. A writer in 1890 had said clams were to this place what the whale had been to Nantucket, and that was still true underneath the parasols.

There was one more thing happening on this water while she trained, and it is the part the postcards leave out.

Prohibition took effect in 1920. New Jersey was the last state to ratify it, in 1922, and the state earned a reputation as a bootlegger's paradise almost immediately. When Rum Row set up offshore of New York Bay, Highlands became a principal port for the trade. The men who fished and clammed and built boats by day ran liquor at night. There were signal lights from the beach. There were hijackers who made their living robbing the smugglers, and there was real violence — a gunfight in the streets of Atlantic Highlands in 1923 left one man dead and more than a dozen shot or jailed. In November 1923, Bill McCoy, the most famous rumrunner on the coast, was finally taken by the Coast Guard off Sandy Hook.

So: a teenage girl swimming a training route from the Hook to the bridge, in a boom-time resort town full of New York day trippers, over water that was being used at night to move illegal liquor into the largest city in the country.

That is the summer of 1926 in Highlands. It was not quiet here.

## What she was swimming against

The Women's Swimming Association of New York was founded in 1917 by Charlotte Epstein, and it was not only a swim club. Its leadership and members campaigned for suffrage, pushed to create swimming events open to women, and worked to get women into the Olympics at all. Ederle joined at twelve.

Her coach there, Louis de Breda Handley, taught her the American crawl and an eight-beat kick that was, at the time, a genuine technical innovation. She was fast almost immediately. Before she turned professional in 1925 she had broken something on the order of twenty-nine national and world amateur records. At the 1924 Paris Olympics she took a gold in the freestyle relay and two individual bronzes.

Understand what she was swimming in. Women's bathing costumes had begun as wool dresses with stockings and shoes, and wet wool does what wet wool does. After her failed 1925 Channel attempt left her chafed raw, Ederle and her sister Meg designed their own suit out of jersey silk — a two-piece with a brassiere top and short trunks. She wore it across the Channel. She believed, later, that it was an ancestor of the modern bikini.

The prevailing view she was swimming against was not subtle. Women were understood to be physiologically incapable of a Channel crossing. Five men had done it. That was the entire list.

In 1925 she turned professional, and she swam from the Battery in lower Manhattan down through the Narrows and out to Sandy Hook. Seven hours and eleven minutes. The record stood for eighty-one years.

Sources disagree on the distance — the swim is variously given as roughly seventeen and a half, twenty-one, or twenty-two miles, which tells you something true about open water, where the tide decides how far you actually went. Her own nephew later described it as a warm-up.

Look at what that swim is, geographically. She swam out of New York City and finished at Sandy Hook. She swam home, to the water she learned in.

## The crossing

Later that same summer she went to France and failed. After eight hours and forty-six minutes her trainer, Jabez Wolffe, had her pulled from the water on the grounds that she was swallowing too much sea. She insisted she could have finished. She fired him and hired Bill Burgess, one of the five men who had actually done it.

She entered the water at Cape Gris-Nez at 7:08 in the morning.

The sea was cold and, at first, unusually calm. Twice that day the weather turned, and twice Burgess called for her to come out. Her father and her sister Meg were in the boat and sided with her. Henry had promised her a red roadster if she finished, and he shouted down at her over the water that the car was only hers if she landed in England.

She landed at Kingsdown, in Kent, at 9:04 that night.

Fourteen hours and thirty-one minutes. The sixth person ever to cross, and the first woman. She had beaten the existing men's record by nearly two hours. The Channel is about twenty-one miles across and the tides pushed her something closer to thirty-five. Burgess, who had done it himself, said afterward that no man or woman had ever made such a swim.

Two million people turned out for her ticker-tape parade in New York. Calvin Coolidge called her America's best girl. She was twenty years old, and for a stretch of that autumn she was the most famous woman in the world.

Her own account of it was plainer than anyone else's. The point, she said, was to show "that a girl could do it, that an American girl could do it."

New York claimed her, immediately and completely, and it has never really let go. They were not the only ones who felt they had a case. The Matawan Journal took a swing at the coverage at the time, noting drily that New York had grabbed all the glory of being her hometown while dismissing this stretch of waterfront as merely the place where the Ederles happened to summer.

The Journal had a point. The Channel is where it happened. Miller Street is where it became possible.

## From the second floor

Here is the part of this we cannot write in the third person.

The steamboats stopped. By 1932 the century-long steamboat operation on the Shrewsbury and the Navesink had ended, and the line that carried a million passengers in her Channel year was finished within a decade. Highland Beach ran on until 1962, when the state took it by eminent domain and the resort came down. The Bamboo Garden is gone. The floating theater is gone. The pier where a butcher lowered his daughter into the river on a clothesline is gone.

The street is still there. The hill is still there. The water is still there.

Our house sits on the upper stretch of that same road — Miller Street below the highway, Woodland above it, one hill with two names, the way towns rename themselves in pieces without ever quite meaning to. There was a third name before those. This slope was Miller Hill, and people lived at addresses on it, and then it wasn't, and now the name is nowhere — off the maps, out of the records, gone the way the Bamboo Garden went.

From the second floor deck, the sightline runs straight down the hill to Miller Beach and out across the bay to the Hook.

Which means that on a clear morning in the summer of 1926, from roughly where we stand with coffee, a person on this hill could have looked down and watched a nineteen-year-old girl swimming the length of that water. Not a celebrity. Not yet. Just the Ederle girl, the butcher's daughter from the city, doing her two hours and forty minutes again, the way she did it the day before.

Nobody would have stopped what they were doing to watch. That is the part that stays with us — background motion to a town busy with steamboat crowds and clam boats and whatever was being moved after dark.

She came back in 1975 for the dedication of a park in this borough named after her. She lived to ninety-eight and died in New Jersey in 2003. Her hearing, damaged at five, eventually failed almost entirely, and she spent years of her later life teaching deaf children to swim — which is either the least surprising fact about her or the most, depending on how closely you have been reading.

A hundred years after the crossing, this town threw her a festival. That is the right instinct. But the thing worth holding onto is smaller and closer than a festival, and it is available to anyone here for free, at any hour.

Stand at the top of the hill. Look down the street. It runs into the bay.

Better: find one of the old peanut stone staircases and take it down. That is the way she went, and the way everyone here went, back when the water at the bottom was the reason you lived at the top.

She did not train somewhere else and visit here. She trained here, in the water at the bottom of our road, and then she went to France and swam faster than every man who had ever tried it.

"People said women couldn't swim the Channel," she said. "I proved they could."

She proved it in France. She learned it at the bottom of this hill.
