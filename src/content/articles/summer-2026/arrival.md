There is a morning in early summer when you step out onto the porch with your coffee and the bay is already busy.

Not the working quiet of an off-season Tuesday, when the water belongs to the ospreys and the fishing boats and one patient man in a skiff.

Busy.

Sailboats leaning into the channel, a charter running out toward the Hook, a paddleboard that has no business being that far from shore, the wake of something you cannot see yet folding against the bulkhead below the hill. Summer does not ease onto this coast. It arrives — on a specific morning, all at once, and you are either watching or you missed it.

We were watching. That is the whole premise of this magazine, and this was the summer that rewarded it.

We have documented three seasons from this hill now, and none of them brought what this one did. The tall ships came — the Sail4th 250 fleet, the largest gathering of its kind in modern memory, and every vessel in it passed the mouth of Sandy Hook Bay before it ever saw Manhattan. We watched them from the bluff and from the water and through the Observatory camera, and we understood, standing there, that no one on the far side of the harbor was seeing what we were seeing. The ships arrived here first. This coast has always been the first door, and for a few days in July the whole world came through it.

The water turned blue. Not the ordinary summer green that comes with the warm weather, but a color this ocean is not supposed to be, for weeks, in front of everyone.

The whales came back. Not to us specifically — they came for the bunker, the way anything with an appetite does — but they came into water that spent forty years quietly becoming worth returning to, and a hundred miles of coastline got to watch it happen. Five hundred of them now, counted one tail at a time.

Fifty people gave up a Sunday evening in June to carry seven hundred pounds of garbage off a spit most of the county loves deeply.

And a hundred years ago this month, a nineteen-year-old girl who learned to swim at the bottom of our street went to France and swam the English Channel faster than any man ever had. She trained here. In this water. Two hours and forty minutes at a time, from the Hook to the bridge, and back again the next day.

That is the thing we did not expect to learn this summer, and it is the reason this issue looks the way it does.

Arrival is not something that happens to a place. Ships arrive. Seasons arrive. Whales arrive. But they arrive whether or not anyone is standing on the shore, and the arriving is only half of it. The other half is the witness — the person on the porch, the volunteer with the bucket, the researcher with the camera, the neighbor who remembers what the street used to be called.

The coast does not need our help being remarkable. It needs someone watching when it is.

Now, you are reading this, which means you are one more person watching, which is the only thing this place has ever really needed.

The season has arrived. We are glad you are here for it.

*— Eric and Mary*
