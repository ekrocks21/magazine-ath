A few days before the cleanup, we walked Plum Island at low tide to see what we were getting into.

The egrets were working the shallows. Horseshoe crabs were moving across the flat in the shallow water the way they have been doing it for something on the order of four hundred million years, which is a number that stops meaning anything if you look at it too long. Two of them had gone over on their backs in the wash and were rowing at the air. We turned them right side up and sent them on.

And sitting in the sand a few feet from all of it was a McDonald's cup.

That is the whole story of this piece, honestly. Everything after it is logistics.

## The evening

On Sunday, June 7, World Ocean Day, we met at the Lot B pavilion at six thirty in the evening and walked out onto Plum Island — the low bay-side spit on the western edge of Sandy Hook, the part of the Hook most people drive past on their way to the ocean without ever knowing it is there.

The cleanup was organized with the Making Waves Coalition, the National Park Service at Gateway, and Monmouth County Clean Communities. Registration was capped and it filled. People came with buckets, gloves, and the specific kind of energy that only exists at golden hour on a Sunday in early June when nobody has anywhere else to be.

Two hours later, the tally came in at roughly seven hundred pounds.

Seven hundred pounds off one narrow spit of bayfront on one evening, collected by hand, by people who volunteered a Sunday night for it.

## What seven hundred pounds is made of

It is worth saying plainly what that weight is made of, because "seven hundred pounds of debris" sounds like it means something dramatic, and mostly it does not. It is not a shipwreck. It is not industrial dumping.

It is the small stuff, in quantity. Bottles and cans. Foam. Rope and monofilament. Bait bags. Cups. Plastic sheeting half buried and pulled out in long strips. Cigarette filters, which weigh almost nothing individually and are therefore among the most numerous things on any beach on earth. Tires, which weigh a great deal and skew any tally that includes them.

Some of it was dropped on that beach by people who were standing on it. Most of it was not. Plum Island sits on the inside of the Hook, at the western hinge of the harbor, which means the bay hands it whatever the bay is carrying — and the bay is fed by the Raritan, the Hudson, the Navesink, the Shrewsbury, and the outflow of the largest metropolitan area in the country. The spit is a filter. It catches things.

The horseshoe crabs come up onto beaches exactly like this one to spawn in late spring, in the shallow bay sand, and their eggs feed migrating shorebirds that have flown from South America and have to make weight before they go north. That is the beach we were standing on with buckets.

## The people

The number is not the part we think about.

Lucas has lived in Monmouth County his entire life. Landon had only just moved here. They ended up on the same stretch of sand with the same buckets, saying more or less the same thing about why they came. That happens at these events with a consistency that has stopped surprising us and started to feel like the actual mechanism.

John, a kite surfer who had come off the water, said the ocean gets him hyped, which is not a sentence anyone would put in a magazine, and is also the truest thing said all evening.

Mary was doing what she always does out there, which is beachcombing for sea glass and shells for her work and filling a second bag with garbage at the same time, because in her mind those have never been two separate activities.

Nadia and the Making Waves crew ran it the way they always run it. Mikayla and Anthony and the Gateway team handled the park side and hauled the weight out. It is not lost on us that the people who do this work most consistently are the ones who get thanked last and quoted least.

## What it actually does

We want to be careful here, because there is a version of this story that flatters everyone involved and teaches nothing.

Seven hundred pounds is two hours on one spit. The tide came in that night and started delivering again. If we went back the following Sunday we would fill bags. The cleanup did not solve marine debris on Sandy Hook Bay any more than sweeping a floor solves dust.

What it does do is smaller and more durable than that. It removes the specific piece of plastic that a specific horseshoe crab would have had to navigate around. It puts forty people on a beach most of them love dearly. It converts an abstraction — the bay is full of garbage — into a bucket with a weight in it, which is a thing a person can actually hold. And it introduces people to each other in a context where the introduction sticks.

We have spent this whole issue writing about arrival. The ships arrived. The season arrived. The whales came back to water that spent forty years becoming worth returning to.

The cup is gone. The crabs are still there.

We will be back to do it again.
